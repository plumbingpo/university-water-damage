Dealing With AC Leak Water Damage in University

All day long an AC system wrings humidity from indoor air, and the resulting water needs a way out. Normally it drips off the coil into a pan and leaves through a small drain line. Algae and dust can plug the line, and then the pan fills up and overflows. If the air handler sits in a hallway closet or the attic, that water ends up in the nearby floor, wall or ceiling.

A slow drip causes more harm than most homeowners would guess. The water wicks into drywall, soaks the platform under the unit and creeps under flooring, often with mold already started by the time a stain shows. Tracking the full extent of the moisture, drying it and making repairs is our part. An HVAC technician still has to fix the unit or unclog the line, and we plan our work around that appointment.

Do This First

Your First Steps

  1. Turn Off the ACSet the thermostat to off, which stops the unit from producing more water. Flip the air handler's breaker too when water is close to its wiring.
  2. Avoid Wet Wiring and LightsDo not touch the unit, nearby outlets or ceiling lights that have water in or around them until power to that area is off.
  3. Clear Water From the PanIf you can reach the pan safely, soak up or wet-vac the standing water so it stops spilling over. Do not crawl across a wet attic to do it.
  4. Soak Up What You CanPress towels into damp carpet, mop hard flooring, put a bucket below any drip from the ceiling, and clear furniture, clothes and boxes out of the closet or away from the stain.
  5. Bring In Two ProsYou need two calls: one to us so we can measure the wet area, one to an HVAC technician who will unclog the drain. Until that repair is done, the unit stays off.
Insurance

Insurance and AC Leak Water Damage

Timing is usually what decides whether an AC leak is covered. A sudden pan overflow that you dealt with promptly is the kind of loss homeowners policies often cover. If the adjuster decides the drip went on for weeks or months, it may be treated as a gradual leak or a maintenance issue and denied. Either way, the cost of fixing the AC unit is typically left out. Our photos and readings are available for your claim, and your policy has the final word.

What We Do

What Happens After You Call

1

Measure the Spread

With thermal imaging and moisture meters, our team checks the unit, the adjacent walls or ceiling and the floor beyond the closet, so wet materials are found even where no stain shows.

2

Our extractors pull water out of the pan, the wood platform and any carpet. Saturated or moldy insulation and drywall are removed, which opens the cavity to dry air.

Common Causes

How It Usually Starts

  • A missing or failed float switch that should have shut the system off.
  • Water escaping from a drain pan that has cracked, rusted through or tilted.
  • More meltwater than the pan can handle after an iced-over coil thaws.
  • Poorly connected drain piping, which lets water out before it reaches the outlet.
  • Units that rely on a condensate pump, when that pump quits or gets unplugged.
Why Speed Matters

What the Water Damages

Mold Near the Return

Damp drywall and wood beside the air handler can grow mold within 24 to 48 hours, and the return air can pull spores into the ductwork and through the house.

Drywall Below the Air Handler

An attic air handler leaks onto the drywall beneath it. First comes a stain, next a sag, and after days of soaking a piece may drop into the bedroom or hall underneath.

The Platform and Closet Walls

In a closet, the unit usually rests on a wooden stand built above the return air box. Water rots that platform and wicks up the surrounding drywall, where it stays hidden behind the unit.

Baseboards and Door Trim

The bottom of the door jamb and the baseboard beside it take on water early. Swollen fiberboard pieces do not recover and are normally swapped for new ones.
