Appliance Leak Water Damage in University: What to Know

Three appliances cause most of the leaks we see. A washing machine supply hose can split and run at full pressure until someone shuts it off. A dishwasher tends to leak slowly from a door seal or drain hose, out of sight under the cabinets. A refrigerator ice maker line is a thin tube that can drip behind the unit for months. Each one needs a different kind of cleanup.

On every University job, the first step is moving the appliance and tracing the path the water took. For a kitchen, the usual places are below the flooring and under the cabinets. In laundry rooms it means the wall behind the machine and the rooms next door, or the ceiling below if the laundry is upstairs. Then our team removes the standing water, meters the materials that soaked it up, and places equipment matched to the size of the damage.

First Five Minutes

Your First Steps

  1. Turn Off the ApplianceStop the cycle and unplug the machine if the outlet and floor around it are dry. Otherwise, use the breaker to cut its power.
  2. Close the Supply ValveWashers have hot and cold valves on the wall behind them. Look below the sink for the valve that feeds a dishwasher. Refrigerator lines have a small valve behind or below.
  3. Mop Up What You CanMop or towel up what you can reach so it stops spreading under cabinets and flooring. Vacuums are safe for this only when built for wet and dry pickup.
  4. Keep the Door ClosedIf the washer or dishwasher has not drained, leave it shut. When the door opens mid-cycle, the full load of water can pour out on the floor.
  5. Photograph the Failed PartKeep the part that failed, whether a hose, a fitting or a valve, and get photos of it. It may be requested by the manufacturer of the appliance and by your insurance company.
The Usual Culprits

How It Usually Starts

  • The drain hose of the washer pulling free of the standpipe, or the standpipe backing up from a clog.
  • Dishwasher parts that wear out, including the door gasket, the drain hose and the seal on the pump.
  • Too much detergent or an overloaded drum that makes the machine overflow.
  • A clogged defrost drain or cracked drain pan that lets a refrigerator leak onto the floor.
  • Rubber washing machine supply hoses that weaken with age and split under pressure.
What Is at Risk

What Happens if You Wait

Kitchen Cabinets

Toe kicks and cabinet sides take on water from underneath when a dishwasher leaks. The panels swell and separate, and wet cabinet boxes can hold moisture against the wall for weeks.

Hidden Mold at the Back

Hardly anyone looks at the dark, warm space behind a dishwasher or refrigerator. A slow drip there feeds mold on the wall and floor long before anyone notices.

Floors and What Is Under Them

Water slides beneath laminate, vinyl plank and hardwood. Cupping and swelling show up in the boards, and the subfloor can stay damp long after the surface appears dry.

Adjoining Rooms

Water may pour from a washer hose for hours on end. It passes under walls into hallways, closets and bedrooms, and soaks carpet and pad well away from the laundry room.

Insurance

What About Insurance?

When a machine overflows unexpectedly or a hose bursts, that is typically treated as sudden and accidental, which many homeowners policies cover. The policy typically pays for the water damage, not for the appliance or the failed part. Gradual leaks usually fall outside coverage, so slow seepage at a dishwasher or an ice maker line makes for a tougher claim. How old the hose was and how it was maintained may also come up with some insurers. Before cleanup, take photos, save the failed part and read your policy.

Pricing

What Affects the Cost

  • Type of water, since gray discharge requires sanitizing and clean supply water does not.
  • Duration of the leak, particularly a hidden drip that went unnoticed behind the machine.
  • Whether mold has started and has to be removed before the area can be dried.
  • Whether cabinets and flooring can be dried in place or need to be removed.
