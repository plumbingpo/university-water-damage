Hardwood Floor Water Damage: What University Homeowners Should Know

Wood floors react to water quickly. Boards take on moisture from below and from the edges, swell against each other and start to cup, with the edges rising higher than the center. A floor in this condition is not necessarily ruined. Solid hardwood that is dried in the first few days often flattens back out. Success depends on a slow, even drying rate that draws water from the flooring and from the subfloor beneath it.

We dry hardwood with floor drying mats. A vacuum unit attached to the sealed mats lifts water out by way of the seams and the wood itself, and dehumidifiers hold the room air at a dry level. This takes longer than drying carpet or drywall, often a week or more. Sanding has to wait until the wood is fully dry. A cupped floor sanded early ends up crowned afterward.

What It Looks Like

  • Board edges sitting higher than the centers, called cupping
  • Crowning, meaning the middle of each board bulges above its edges
  • Creaking and popping noises from boards that used to be quiet
  • A hazy, whitened or flaking finish where the water sat
  • Boards lifting off the subfloor or tenting upward at the seams
  • Flooring that gives under your weight in the area that got wet

What We Do

Our Hardwood Floor Water Damage Process

1

Take Baseline Readings

Our team meters the wet boards, the subfloor under them and a dry area of floor to compare against. The comparison shows how far down moisture reached and how likely the floor is to recover.

2

Extract Standing Water

Standing water is extracted right away and rugs and furniture are moved off the wet area. Boards swell less when the seams stop taking in water sooner.

3

Set Drying Mats

We tape drying mats to the floor and attach them to a vacuum system, which draws moisture upward through the wood. The room is managed with dehumidifiers so drying stays even and the boards do not crack.

4

Monitor and Advise

Every day or two we take new readings, continuing until the subfloor and boards match the dry reference spots. Our final advice is one of three outcomes, no further work, swapping a few boards, or refinishing the floor.

What You Cannot See

The Damage You Do Not See

Mold Under Boards

No air moves and no light reaches the gap between the hardwood and the subfloor. Mold in that layer cannot be seen from the room, and a musty odor is usually the first sign.

Water That Keeps Moving

Once below the boards, water moves. Cabinets and wall plates do not stop it, so it may enter adjacent rooms or come out at the ceiling one floor down.

Sanding Too Early

Leveling a damp, cupped floor with a sander takes wood off the lifted edges. After the moisture leaves, each board is left with a raised center, which is crowning.

Wet Subfloor

Long after the boards seem dry, the subfloor may still be holding water. Leave it wet and the cupping returns or mold grows up from below.

If Nothing Is Done

What Happens if You Wait

The first few hours

Seepage Below the Boards

Water slips through the seams and spreads between the boards and subfloor. You can towel off the top and still have a saturated underside.

24 to 48 hours

Cupping Starts

Boards swell and the edges rise. Finish may turn cloudy. Growth is able to begin below, on raw wood and on the subfloor panels.

Days 3 through 7

Boards Start Lifting

Swelling builds pressure across the floor. Edges get crushed together, nails work loose and parts of the floor may start rising from the subfloor.

Past one week

Beyond Drying

Buckling, black staining and mold under the boards become likely. At this stage most of the wet area needs to be replaced rather than dried.
